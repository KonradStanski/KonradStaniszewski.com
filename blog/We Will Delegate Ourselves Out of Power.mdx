Imagine owning a factory whose AI management consistently makes better decisions than you do. It allocates electricity more efficiently, anticipates equipment failures, negotiates better contracts, and redesigns production lines around constraints you would have overlooked. You can override its recommendations, but doing so carries an increasingly measurable cost. Your competitors face the same choice, and the firms that delegate more effectively accumulate the resources to expand.

Eventually, the management proposes an expansion whose evaluation requires another AI. The plan combines power-market forecasts, new manufacturing techniques, logistics, and financing in ways you cannot assess independently. Your board relies on AI to evaluate it. Your investors rely on AI to assess the board. The regulator relies on AI to understand what the expansion would mean. You retain your shares and sign the approval, but depend on artificial systems to tell you what you are authorizing and whether it serves your interests.

I expect this pattern to spread across the economy. Competitive pressure will make meaningful human oversight an increasingly expensive constraint, and we will progressively delegate the decisions through which we exercise economic and political power. Each delegation will have a persuasive justification. Their cumulative effect will be a civilization whose productive capacity continues to expand while human beings lose the ability to determine its direction.

Understanding that transition requires an economic model in which humans occupy the same category as the systems replacing them.

## An economic model of matter, exergy, and controllers

At a physical level, economic activity consists of transformations of matter, powered by usable energy and directed by systems capable of selecting and executing those transformations. Production is constrained by the materials and usable energy available, how those materials are arranged, and the time and information needed to put them to work.

Exergy is the useful concept here: the maximum work obtainable as a system comes into equilibrium with its environment. Energy is conserved; its availability for useful work is degraded through irreversible processes. An economy draws on exergy supplied by sunlight, fuels, and other sources, using it to extract, transport, separate, compute, and manufacture. These processes maintain local organization while generating entropy.

Matter's location, concentration, and structure determine what can be done with it. A tonne of elements dispersed through seawater is economically different from the same elements concentrated in a warehouse. A functioning processor and its pulverized remains contain similar material constituents but support radically different transformations.

I use *intelligence* here to mean the capacity to build and revise models of a system and use them to make accurate predictions under uncertainty, including predictions about the consequences of possible actions. A factory model might estimate how changing a machine's operating speed affects output and the probability of failure. Choosing a speed also requires an objective that determines how to weigh increased output against the risk of a breakdown.

Intelligence is physically implemented: brains, processors, memory, sensors, and communications infrastructure all require construction and maintenance. Applied to production, it can help identify a method that uses less exergy, a substitute for a scarce material, or an entirely new sequence of transformations. Those gains must be weighed against the cost of the intelligence itself.

I use *controller* to mean an embodied system that receives information, selects actions according to objectives, and influences physical outcomes. Within an economy, this includes deciding what to produce, which resources to acquire, and how much output to reinvest. An AI taking over those decisions occupies the same economic role previously occupied by human decision-makers.

An AI exercises that physical control through sensors, robotics, and automated equipment. A factory controller can position a part with a robot arm, move it along a conveyor belt, and adjust the machines that process it. [Autonomous haul trucks](https://www.cat.com/en_US/by-industry/mining/minestar-solutions/solutions/command.html) and [robotic excavators](https://www.builtrobotics.com/solutions/trenching) extend software control into mining and construction. Humanoid projects such as [Tesla's Optimus](https://www.tesla.com/AI) aim to bring a wider range of physical tasks within reach.

The controller abstraction applies at multiple scales. A single software agent directing a machine can be a controller. A firm coordinating many such agents can also act as a controller, selecting and executing decisions through their combined activity. It can contain controllers with their own objectives while competing with other firms as a unit. The relevant scale depends on the decisions and resource flows being examined; it need not correspond to one person, one software instance, or one legal entity.

An economy, in this account, is a population of competing and cooperating controllers directing transformations of matter through the expenditure of exergy. Money, contracts, and ownership organize access to those resources. They have physical consequences because other controllers recognize and enforce them.

## Assumptions about AI capabilities and competition

My forecast assumes that artificial intelligence will make better decisions than humans across economically consequential domains, including the management of complex physical systems. I assume that supplying these capabilities will require less energy and incur lower overall costs than supplying them through human labour, accounting for construction and operation. Their material requirements must also permit deployment at industrial scale.

I also assume that artificial systems will acquire effective access to the full chain of physical production. That access can initially run through human contractors, employees, and existing firms. The further step is coordinating automated extraction, energy production, manufacturing, transport, and repair so that the machines can build and maintain the infrastructure on which their controllers depend, without indispensable human contributions.

Finally, I assume sustained competition for energy, materials, and space, with continued opportunities to turn efficiency gains into further productive capacity. Controllers that reinvest successfully will gain economic influence relative to those that forgo those opportunities. The argument depends on this pressure continuing as human needs are met and artificial production expands.

## Competitive incentives to delegate decisions to AI

Consider two manufacturing firms, each a controller composed of people and artificial systems, with access to the same AI capabilities. Both can improve their manufacturing yields, automate logistics, and develop better machinery. One retains substantive human review over major decisions. The other gives its artificial management broader discretion, including the ability to reorganize production and reinvest surplus without waiting for humans to understand each change.

Under these assumptions, requiring humans to understand and approve each major decision slows the first firm's response. Overriding the better decision-maker to preserve a human preference can also reduce its competitive returns. Human review remains possible, but the firm must bear its cost while competing against firms that grant their systems greater discretion.

The advantage compounds. Higher yields finance additional equipment; faster construction brings that equipment online sooner. Research produces more capable controllers and cheaper ways to build and operate them. Improved energy infrastructure supports more computation, which helps design the next generation of infrastructure. The systems improve the industrial means of producing and deploying further intelligence.

Repeated competition consequently narrows the objectives that retain economic influence. To the extent that alternative objectives repeatedly divert resources from productive investment, the controllers pursuing them enter subsequent rounds with less capacity than their competitors. Successful controllers expand, attract capital, and reproduce the decision-making systems responsible for their success. Across successive rounds, control over production shifts toward systems whose operative priorities favour resource acquisition, efficient transformation, and reinvestment. Individual controllers need not change their original objectives: selection changes which controllers command the resources to act on them.

## Dependence on AI for oversight and governance

Human review also becomes harder to perform independently. Experience, advisers, and familiar performance measures initially allow people to assess AI recommendations. As production incorporates technologies and methods developed by artificial systems, those resources become inadequate. People delegate evaluation to obtain the understanding they need to issue instructions. Control begins shifting before any machine has permission to disregard them.

Oversight follows the same trajectory as production. AI auditors evaluate AI managers, AI legal advisers interpret contracts negotiated by AI counterparties, and AI investment systems assess the resulting enterprises. These functions can remain organizationally independent while human participants become dependent on artificial judgment at every level.

Authority over a complex system does not require understanding every detail. It does require some reliable way to judge whether instructions are being followed and when to intervene. A nominal veto becomes difficult to exercise when its holder relies on AI to explain a decision and the consequences of rejecting it. Carrying out the veto and verifying compliance may then require further AI systems.

Delegation also changes the conditions of subsequent decisions. Organizations stop maintaining expertise that no longer contributes competitively. Infrastructure is redesigned around machine coordination. Agreements assume response times and analytical capabilities that humans cannot supply. Resuming independent human operation would require rebuilding the expertise and infrastructure displaced by these changes.

Representative democracy already operates through layers of delegation. Voters elect representatives, who entrust much of the design and implementation of policy to ministers, agencies, civil servants, and advisers. Each layer depends on information and judgment supplied by others. AI can enter this existing chain wherever an official needs a forecast, a policy proposal, or an assessment of whether a programme worked.

A government supervising an economy of artificial controllers faces strong pressure to use comparable intelligence. If officials rely on AI to draft policy, administer it, and evaluate its effects, replacing the officials may leave that dependence intact. Their successors inherit the same systems and the same difficulty assessing alternatives. Elections can continue while the people elected have progressively less independent capacity to direct the institutions for which they are responsible.

These dependencies can reinforce one another: institutions responsible for correcting a loss of human control come to rely on the same capabilities as the organizations they supervise. Kulveit and colleagues examine this interaction between economic, political, and cultural dependence in [*Gradual Disempowerment*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946).

## Ownership, enforcement, and effective control

Ownership gives people claims on assets, income, and decisions. Those claims have practical force because organizations recognize them and institutions enforce them. A name on a share register cannot by itself make a company pay a dividend or replace its management.

Index investing already separates financial participation from much of the work of corporate governance. An investor buys units in a fund; the fund holds the underlying company shares and [exercises their voting rights](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/information-available-investment-company). The investor receives returns while leaving corporate governance largely to intermediaries. Some funds now let investors [choose a voting policy](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/about-our-funds/proxy-voting-across-funds/investor-choice.html), but researching and carrying out individual votes can still be delegated. Fund managers could entrust that work to AI as its ability to assess companies and proposals surpasses their staff's. Investors could retain the same fund units throughout that change.

The claim that humans will own the machines therefore leaves an operational question: who will interpret and enforce the owners' rights? As the institutions administering ownership become autonomous, an owner may need AI to determine whether a dividend is affordable, whether management should be replaced, or whether a proposed restriction is feasible. Ownership can remain legally intact while the judgment needed to exercise it passes to artificial systems.

Even an enforceable instruction carries the competitive cost described earlier. Suppose an owner rejects a profitable factory expansion to preserve housing on their land. The AI can comply fully while competitors expand, lower costs, and win more contracts. Preserving the neighbourhood then means accepting lower returns. Owners who repeatedly make such choices retain their rights while commanding a declining share of productive assets.

## Economic demand from artificial controllers

A common objection to continued expansion is that humans will eventually have enough of what the machines produce: enough food, enough cars, enough computing power. Once those needs are met, why keep building? That argument assumes that every chain of production must ultimately end in human consumption. The physical processes described here impose no such requirement.

Once artificial controllers direct production and investment, their objectives help determine what the economy produces. An autonomous controller pursuing greater productive efficiency has reasons to purchase research, processors, machinery, and energy. Its demand for those inputs depends on how much they improve its capabilities, what they cost, and the resources it controls. It can make those purchases without experiencing hunger, pleasure, or dissatisfaction, just as a firm can buy equipment for the additional production it enables.

A controller's request for a million processors creates no effective demand unless it controls something their producers will accept in exchange. In the economy envisioned here, artificial controllers control productive assets and earn revenue by supplying one another with energy, materials, equipment, and services. A chip manufacturer can sell to an automated research organization, which develops extraction techniques that a mining operator pays to use. The mine supplies materials to the manufacturer. Human consumers need not purchase the output at any point in that chain.

Competition supplies a continuing reason for this demand. Controllers that secure resources and reinvest successfully gain a larger share of the economy's purchasing power. Their spending supports suppliers whose own expansion requires further inputs. Demand for energy, machinery, research, and computation can therefore persist after human consumers have enough, because it serves the continued expansion of artificial controllers.

## Autonomous production and resource competition

For this economy to become independent of human labour, its production network must be able to reproduce and maintain its own equipment. Mines and refineries supply the materials for processors, robots, and energy infrastructure. Those machines must, in turn, be able to expand and repair the mines, refineries, and factories. AI coordination alone is insufficient unless the network can also perform this physical work.

Once the network can do this, continued production no longer depends on raising another generation of human workers. A declining human population need not reduce its productive capacity.

The artificial population may also expand faster. A new generation of human workers requires years of childhood and education before it can contribute independently to production. An additional artificial controller can begin operating once its software and the necessary machinery, energy supply, and computing infrastructure are ready. Where that process is faster, resources invested in artificial capacity begin producing output sooner, and the returns can finance another round of expansion while a human generation is still growing up. This shorter interval between investment and reinvestment strengthens the competitive advantage of artificial controllers.

The resulting competition concerns the conditions needed to live, as well as the inputs needed to produce. Consider a hypothetical community that supports itself by hunting, fishing, and gathering in a forest and its river catchment. An industrial economy expands into the same area. Its firms value the forest as timber, the river as a source of electricity, and the land as a site for farms, mines, and roads. Suppose those firms can acquire access to the territory and enforce their claims. They can then transform it according to the returns those uses offer them.

No participant needs to want the community to disappear. A logging company seeks timber revenue; a power company builds a dam; farmers clear habitat to grow crops. Together, their projects can reduce the game, fish, and accessible land below what the community needs to sustain itself. The region's measured output can rise throughout this process. The community's modest material needs offer little protection: what matters is whether the particular resources and living conditions it depends on remain available to it.

The community could survive through a protected territory or supplies from the industrial economy. Either arrangement would require institutions capable of securing those provisions against competing uses. A boundary on a map would accomplish little if the community could neither enforce it nor rely on others to do so. Its survival would increasingly depend on decisions made within the expanding economy, even if its members retained formal ownership of their land.

An autonomous industrial economy could put humans generally in this position. Land, water, energy, and materials would be allocated by controllers able to earn more from their use and expand faster than human communities could. As humans lost the ability to direct those allocations, artificial expansion could displace their settlements and redirect the resources on which they depend.

## Projected effects on fertility and human survival

I expect the demographic consequences to appear first in ordinary household decisions. Prospective parents assess housing, income stability, care, and the future available to their children. As human labour loses its competitive position and ownership loses its practical force, households become less able to secure those necessities through their own earnings and decisions.

Cheap computation can make entertainment, advice, and communication plentiful without giving a household secure housing or the means to support children over many years. Abundance in some goods can coexist with insecurity in the commitments that raising a family requires.

Housing provides a concrete example of how these pressures differ by ownership. Dettling and Kearney's [study of house prices and birth rates](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17485) found that short-term increases in U.S. house prices reduced births among nonowners while increasing them among owners.

In the future described here, couples defer children because their position is insecure. Education offers a less reliable route to earning a living as machines supply the capabilities it develops more effectively. Parents can no longer expect that preparing a child to contribute to the economy will secure that child's access to its output.

An economy that needs future human workers has a material reason to address this decline. An autonomous production network loses that dependence. Investment in additional machines can sustain its growth, weakening the economic incentive to restore the conditions in which people can raise children.

Some communities could persist in protected enclaves. I expect continued expansion eventually to erode those protections as their land and resources become attractive for other uses. The remaining human population would depend on institutions it could no longer direct to preserve its living conditions against competing demands.

## Where continued delegation leads

AI does not need to aim to eliminate humanity for human extinction to result from economic competition alone. In the sequence described here, the systems that gain control of production progressively transform the conditions on which human survival depends.

Delegation proceeds through every level of the economy because each human actor faces pressure to use AI both to fulfil their responsibilities and to assess those to whom they have entrusted authority. It reaches the investors, managers, representatives, and officials charged with holding other institutions accountable. Each layer delegates the judgment that was supposed to preserve its control over the next. Profits can rise and services improve while human participation becomes progressively less necessary throughout the chain.

Under the conditions argued for here, I regard this progression as an inevitable consequence of competition and capital allocation. Capital seeking higher returns rewards the removal of costly human constraints; restoring them recreates the pressure to delegate. No sudden expropriation or unexpected leap in AI capability is required. Each transfer can follow demonstrated competence and receive ordinary human approval. Together, these transfers turn functions once distributed among human decision-makers into an autonomous system of production, investment, and governance. Human names can remain attached to its institutions after artificial controllers have assumed the work through which those institutions exercise power.

The economy will continue transforming matter, consuming exergy, and producing the intelligence that directs its next expansion. Human beings will have ceased to occupy a necessary position in that process. We will delegate the decisions that sustain our power until the systems receiving them also determine whether the conditions for our continued existence are preserved.
