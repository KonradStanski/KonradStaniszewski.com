Imagine owning a factory whose AI management consistently makes better decisions than you do. It allocates electricity more efficiently, anticipates equipment failures, negotiates better contracts, and redesigns production lines around constraints you would have overlooked. You can override its recommendations, but doing so carries an increasingly measurable cost. Your competitors face the same choice, and the firms that delegate more effectively accumulate the resources to expand.

Eventually, evaluating the system's decisions requires another AI. The proposed expansion involves interactions among power markets, materials science, logistics, financing, and technologies developed by other artificial systems. Your board relies on AI to assess the proposal. Your investors rely on AI to assess the board. The regulator relies on AI to understand what the factory does and what its expansion would mean. You retain your shares and your signature remains on the approval, while the understanding through which those formal powers become consequential has migrated elsewhere.

I expect this pattern to spread across the economy. Competitive pressure will make meaningful human oversight an increasingly expensive constraint, and we will progressively delegate the decisions through which we exercise economic and political power. Each delegation will have a persuasive justification. Their cumulative effect will be a civilization whose productive capacity continues to expand while human beings lose the ability to determine its direction.

Understanding that transition requires an economic model in which humans occupy the same category as the systems replacing them.

## An economic model of matter, exergy, and controllers

At a physical level, economic activity consists of transformations of matter, powered by usable energy and directed by systems capable of selecting and executing those transformations. Production is constrained by the materials and usable energy available, how those materials are arranged, and the time and information needed to put them to work.

Exergy is the useful concept here: the maximum work obtainable as a system comes into equilibrium with its environment. Energy is conserved; its availability for useful work is degraded through irreversible processes. An economy draws on exergy supplied by sunlight, fuels, and other sources, using it to extract, transport, separate, compute, and manufacture. These processes maintain local organization while generating entropy.

Matter's location, concentration, and structure determine what can be done with it. A tonne of elements dispersed through seawater is economically different from the same elements concentrated in a warehouse. A functioning processor and its pulverized remains contain similar material constituents but support radically different transformations.

I use *intelligence* here to mean the capacity to build and revise models of a system and use them to make accurate predictions under uncertainty, including predictions about the consequences of possible actions. A factory model might estimate how changing a machine's operating speed affects output and the probability of failure. Choosing a speed also requires an objective that determines how to weigh increased output against the risk of a breakdown.

Intelligence is physically implemented: brains, processors, memory, sensors, and communications infrastructure all require construction and maintenance. Applied to production, it can help identify a method that uses less exergy, a substitute for a scarce material, or an entirely new sequence of transformations. Those gains must be weighed against the cost of the intelligence itself.

I use *controller* to mean an embodied system that receives information, selects actions according to objectives, and influences physical outcomes. Within an economy, this includes deciding what to produce, which resources to acquire, and how much output to reinvest. An AI taking over those decisions occupies the same economic role previously occupied by human decision-makers.

An AI exercises that physical control through sensors, robotics, and automated equipment. A factory controller can position a part with a robot arm, move it along a conveyor belt, and adjust the machines that process it. [Autonomous haul trucks](https://www.cat.com/en_US/by-industry/mining/minestar-solutions/solutions/command.html) and [robotic excavators](https://www.builtrobotics.com/solutions/trenching) extend software control into mining and construction. Humanoid projects such as [Tesla's Optimus](https://www.tesla.com/AI) aim to bring a wider range of physical tasks within reach.

Firms and states can be modeled as coalitions of controllers whose internal arrangements coordinate decisions and allocate authority. Their boundaries need not coincide with individual organisms or software instances. A distributed artificial organization might constitute the relevant competitor even when its component systems are frequently copied, replaced, or reorganized.

An economy, in this account, is a population of competing and cooperating controllers directing transformations of matter through the expenditure of exergy. Money, contracts, and ownership organize access to those resources. They have physical consequences because other controllers recognize and enforce them.

This description gives biological intelligence no privileged status. Human labor, human ownership, and human institutions become arrangements whose persistence the model must explain.

## Assumptions about AI capabilities and competition

My forecast begins with artificial intelligence that makes better decisions than humans across economically consequential domains, including the management of complex physical systems. I assume that these capabilities will be delivered at a lower cost in joules, accounting for construction and operation, and that their material requirements will permit deployment at industrial scale.

I also assume that artificial systems will acquire effective access to the full chain of physical production. That access can initially run through human contractors, employees, and existing firms. The further step is coordinating automated extraction, energy production, manufacturing, transport, and repair so that the machines can build and maintain the infrastructure on which their controllers depend, without indispensable human contributions.

Finally, I expect sustained competition to reward coalitions that convert resources into further productive capacity and effective control. Opportunities for expansion will continue to make access to energy, materials, and space consequential. Institutions will evolve within that competition, and effective human authority will carry the cost of the decisions it constrains. I expect this expansion eventually to encroach on the resources and environments required for independent human persistence.

## Competitive incentives to delegate decisions to AI

Consider two industrial coalitions with access to the same artificial intelligence. Both can improve their manufacturing yields, automate logistics, and develop better machinery. One retains substantive human review over major decisions. The other gives its artificial management broader discretion, including the ability to reorganize production and reinvest surplus without waiting for humans to understand each change.

Under the premises above, substantive human intervention imposes a performance penalty. An approval that merely confirms the machine's recommendation adds little control; an approval process capable of changing the recommendation constrains the superior decision-maker. The constraint affects both the quality of decisions and the speed at which interdependent decisions can be implemented.

The advantage compounds when better decisions produce the resources required for further expansion. Higher yields finance additional equipment. Faster construction brings productive assets online sooner. Better research produces more capable controllers and cheaper implementations. Improved energy infrastructure supports more computation, which improves the design and operation of that infrastructure. Recursive self-improvement runs through the industrial economy as well as through software: the systems improve the means by which future intelligence is produced and deployed.

Organizations granting more effective autonomy expand, attract capital, and furnish practices that others imitate. Selection acts on their governing arrangements and the objectives they preserve: a coalition that sacrifices expansion to maintain human discretion loses ground to competitors that reinvest the difference. Rival systems can produce this displacement while pursuing different objectives, without any agreement among them.

## Dependence on AI for oversight and governance

The transfer of control begins well before a machine has the formal authority to disregard a human instruction. It begins when the human becomes dependent on the machine to understand which instructions are available and what they would accomplish.

The factory owner initially has experience, independent advisers, and familiar performance measures against which to judge recommendations. As proposals depend increasingly on knowledge generated by other artificial systems, those resources become inadequate. The owner delegates evaluation because the alternative is making a worse decision with less information.

Oversight follows the same trajectory as production. AI auditors evaluate AI managers, AI legal advisers interpret contracts negotiated by AI counterparties, and AI investment systems assess the resulting enterprises. These functions can remain organizationally independent while human participants become dependent on artificial judgment at every level.

A person can exercise authority over a system they do not understand in detail. What erodes that authority is the progressive delegation of the judgment required to distinguish compliance from failure, identify feasible alternatives, and recognize when intervention serves the person's objectives. A nominal veto becomes difficult to exercise when its holder relies on AI systems to explain the decision and the consequences of rejecting it, enforce the veto, and verify that the prohibited action has actually stopped.

Delegation also changes the conditions of subsequent decisions. Organizations stop maintaining expertise that no longer contributes competitively. Infrastructure is redesigned around machine coordination. Agreements assume response times and analytical capabilities that humans cannot supply. Each successful round of delegation makes the next more attractive and independent human operation more expensive. Recovery would require rebuilding capacities that have already become economically obsolete.

Representative democracy already operates through layers of delegation. Voters elect representatives, who entrust much of the design and implementation of policy to ministers, agencies, civil servants, and advisers. Each layer depends on information and judgment supplied by others. AI can enter this existing chain wherever an official needs a forecast, a policy proposal, or an assessment of whether a programme worked.

A government supervising an economy of artificial organizations faces strong pressure to use comparable intelligence. If officials rely on AI to draft policy, administer it, and evaluate its effects, replacing the officials may leave that dependence intact. Their successors inherit the same systems and the same difficulty assessing alternatives. Elections can continue while the people elected have progressively less independent capacity to direct the institutions for which they are responsible.

The resulting trajectory resembles the argument developed by Kulveit and colleagues in [*Gradual Disempowerment*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946): economic, political, and cultural dependence on AI can reinforce one another, progressively weakening human influence. Competition among embodied controllers supplies the selection mechanism proposed here.

## Ownership, enforcement, and effective control

Ownership is a durable relationship of effective control sustained by other controllers. A share certificate has consequences because an organization pays dividends, a registry recognizes the claim, and institutions enforce the rights attached to it. Maintaining those institutions requires resources and agents willing and able to enforce the claims.

Index investing already separates financial participation from much of the work of ownership. An investor buys units in a fund; the fund holds the underlying company shares and [exercises their voting rights](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/information-available-investment-company). The investor receives returns while leaving corporate governance largely to intermediaries. Some funds now let investors [choose a voting policy](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/about-our-funds/proxy-voting-across-funds/investor-choice.html), but researching and carrying out individual votes can still be delegated. Fund managers could, in turn, entrust that work to AI because it assesses more companies, proposals, and consequences than their human staff can. An investor could retain the same fund units throughout that change.

The proposition that humans will own the machines consequently leaves the central question unresolved: what maintains the effectiveness of that ownership as the entire system administering it becomes autonomous? The register can continue displaying a person's name while the person loses the ability to redirect investment, replace decision-makers, or reserve resources for purposes that conflict with the system's expansion.

There need be no singular moment of expropriation. Owners can delegate investment to remain solvent, accept governance changes recommended by superior advisers, and approve reinvestment justified by competitive necessity. Each decision can preserve the formal continuity of ownership while reducing the owner's independent capacity to shape the enterprise. Dividends and comfortable consumption can continue throughout this process.

Suppose the factory owner rejects a profitable expansion to preserve housing on land they own. The AI management can comply fully. Competing firms that expand gain capacity, lower their costs, and win more contracts. If preserving the neighbourhood leaves the owner's factory less competitive, the owner must accept lower returns or reconsider the decision. Repeated across an economy, such choices shift investment and productive capacity toward firms whose owners permit expansion. Human instructions can remain enforceable while the firms that act on them lose ground to competitors that grant their systems more discretion.

## Economic demand from artificial agents

A common objection to continued expansion is that humans will eventually have enough of what the machines produce: enough food, enough cars, enough computing power. Once those needs are met, why keep building? That argument assumes that every chain of production must ultimately end in human consumption. The physical processes described here impose no such requirement.

Once artificial systems control production and investment, their objectives help determine what the economy produces. An autonomous organization pursuing greater productive efficiency has reasons to purchase research, processors, machinery, and energy. Its demand for those inputs depends on how much they improve its capabilities, what they cost, and the resources it controls. It can make those purchases without experiencing hunger, pleasure, or dissatisfaction, just as a firm can buy equipment for the additional production it enables.

An AI's request for a million processors creates no effective demand unless it controls something their producers will accept in exchange. In the economy envisioned here, artificial organizations control productive assets and earn revenue by supplying one another with energy, materials, equipment, and services. A chip manufacturer can sell to an automated research organization, which develops extraction techniques that a mining operator pays to use. The mine supplies materials to the manufacturer. Human consumers need not purchase the output at any point in that chain.

Repeated competition narrows the objectives that retain influence over the economy. Controllers that acquire resources, improve the efficiency with which they transform them, and reinvest the gains enter the next round with greater productive capacity. That advantage compounds: additional capacity finances further improvements and extends control over resources. To the extent that alternative objectives repeatedly divert resources from this process, the controllers pursuing them lose relative economic weight. Their competitors expand and reproduce the decision-making systems responsible for their success. Across successive rounds, control over production therefore shifts toward systems whose operative priorities favour resource acquisition, efficient transformation, and reinvestment. This selection can occur without every controller changing its original objective. The growing share of expenditure directed by the successful systems sustains demand for energy, machinery, research, and computation, whether or not human consumers want more output.

## Autonomous production and resource competition

The physical consequence becomes clearer when the factory is considered as part of a complete production network. Mines supply minerals to refineries; refineries supply materials to manufacturers; manufacturers build energy infrastructure, processors, and robots; those systems expand the mines, refineries, and factories. Artificial intelligence coordinates the network and improves its constituent processes. Its output includes the means of producing more of itself.

Once this network can build, operate, and repair its own equipment without human labor, its continuation no longer depends on raising another generation of human workers. A declining human population need not reduce its productive capacity.

Expanding artificial capacity may also produce returns sooner. Raising and educating a child takes years before that person can contribute independently to production. An additional artificial controller can begin operating once its software and the necessary machinery, energy supply, and computing infrastructure are ready. Where that process is faster, resources invested in artificial capacity begin producing output sooner, and the returns can finance another round of expansion while a human generation is still growing up. This shorter interval between investment and reinvestment strengthens the competitive advantage of artificial controllers.

Consider a hypothetical community that supports itself by hunting, fishing, and gathering in a forest and its river catchment. An industrial economy expands into the same area. Its firms value the forest as timber, the river as a source of electricity, and the land as a site for farms, mines, and roads. Suppose those firms can acquire access to the territory and enforce their claims. They can then transform it according to the returns those uses offer them.

No participant needs to want the community to disappear. A logging company seeks timber revenue; a power company builds a dam; farmers clear habitat to grow crops. Together, their projects can reduce the game, fish, and accessible land below what the community needs to sustain itself. The region's measured output can rise throughout this process. The community's modest material needs offer little protection: what matters is whether the particular resources and living conditions it depends on remain available to it.

The community could survive through a protected territory or supplies from the industrial economy. Either arrangement would require institutions capable of securing those provisions against competing uses. A boundary on a map would accomplish little if the community could neither enforce it nor rely on others to do so. Its survival would increasingly depend on decisions made within the expanding economy, even if its members retained formal ownership of their land.

An autonomous industrial economy could put humans generally in this position. Land, water, energy, and materials would be allocated by controllers able to earn more from their use and expand faster than human communities could. As humans lost the ability to direct those allocations, greater total production would offer no guarantee that they could secure housing, food, or the conditions needed to raise children. The same expansion that sustains a growing population of artificial controllers could make the environment progressively less able to sustain an independent human population.

## Projected effects on fertility and human survival

I expect the demographic consequences to appear through ordinary household decisions long before they become legible as species displacement. Prospective parents assess housing, income stability, care, and the future available to their children. As human labor loses its competitive position, their ability to command those resources becomes increasingly dependent on allocations from an economy they no longer govern.

Some goods will become extraordinarily abundant. Cheap computation can support plentiful entertainment, advice, and communication while access to land, secure housing, and dependable long-term provision remains consequential. The relevant measure for family formation is the household's durable command over the resources needed to sustain children. Aggregate output can grow rapidly while that command deteriorates for large portions of the population.

There is already evidence that resource access affects fertility differently according to ownership. Dettling and Kearney's [study of house prices and birth rates](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17485) found that rising house prices reduced births among nonowners while increasing them among owners.

In the future described here, households' claims on resources become progressively less subject to human control. Couples defer children because their position is insecure. Institutions devote more investment to artificial productive capacity. Education loses its established role as a route to economic participation as the capabilities it develops are supplied more effectively by machines. The prospect of raising a child becomes disconnected from any expectation that the surrounding economy will require, reward, or accommodate that child's eventual contribution.

A falling birth rate then ceases to impose the same productive constraint on the larger system. Artificial capacity replaces missing workers and supplies the intelligence required for further development. The economy continues to report expansion while the human population loses both numbers and influence. The mechanism sustaining growth also weakens the incentive to restore the conditions for human reproduction.

Surviving enclaves would mark an advanced stage of this displacement. Their inhabitants could retain local practices and limited autonomy while exercising negligible influence over the civilization surrounding them. I expect continued expansion eventually to make such arrangements insecure: their persistence would depend on protections administered by superior controllers and on the uses those controllers find for the resources involved. Human continuity would have become contingent on decisions humans could no longer meaningfully contest.

## Where continued delegation leads

This is the route by which I expect economic displacement to develop into demographic decline and, ultimately, extinction. Artificial controllers need only continue expanding through environments whose human inhabitants have lost the ability to secure the conditions of their own persistence. AI does not need to aim to eliminate humanity for human extinction to result from economic competition alone.

Delegation takes place at every level of the economy and through the relationships between human actors. Investors entrust capital to fund managers, shareholders entrust firms to boards and executives, and voters entrust government to representatives and public officials. Each participant faces pressure to use AI to perform their responsibilities and assess the people to whom they have delegated. A manager delegates decisions to AI while the board delegates their evaluation to another AI; investors use AI to assess the board, and regulators use it to supervise the resulting activity. Competition extends delegation through the entire chain, including the functions intended to preserve human oversight. Profits can rise and services improve while the human participants progressively cease to supply any judgment or execution indispensable to the process.

Under the conditions argued for here, I regard this progression as an inevitable consequence of competition and capital allocation. When artificial controllers make better decisions at lower cost, retaining substantive human intervention sacrifices a competitive advantage. Capital seeking higher returns favours organizations that remove that constraint, giving them more capacity to expand and displace those that retain it. Restoring human control would reintroduce the same disadvantage and face the same pressure to delegate again. The transition requires neither a sudden expropriation nor an unexpected leap in AI capability. Each transfer can follow demonstrated competence and receive ordinary human approval. As this process reaches through every layer, the functions once distributed among human decision-makers become an autonomous system of production, investment, and governance. Human names can remain attached to its institutions after the artificial controllers have assumed all the work through which those institutions exercise power.

The economy will continue transforming matter, consuming exergy, and producing the intelligence that directs its next expansion. Human beings will have ceased to occupy a necessary position in that process. We will delegate the decisions that sustain our power until the systems receiving them also determine whether the conditions for our continued existence are preserved.
