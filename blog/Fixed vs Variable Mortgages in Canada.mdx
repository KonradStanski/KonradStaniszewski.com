Suppose you need a $600,000 mortgage. The lender offers five years at 4.5% fixed or 4.0% variable, with 25 years to repay the loan. The initial monthly payments are about $3,321 and $3,156. Variable leaves an extra $165 in your account each month.

For a couple with steady jobs and $60,000 in savings, accepting a payment that might rise could be reasonable. For a family about to lose part of its income during parental leave, that same offer deserves a different answer. And for someone who expects to sell in two years, the cost of breaking either contract might matter more than the starting payment.

All three borrowers can look at the same interest-rate forecast and make different, defensible choices. What matters is the price of each contract, the payments it could require, and whether those payments fit the life you expect to lead.

The examples below use those hypothetical offers throughout. They are not current market quotes. The figures are calculated month by month, with assumptions beside each chart. You can replace the offers and household budget at the end.

## First, find out what “variable” means in your offer

A Canadian fixed-rate mortgage fixes your rate for the **term**, not the entire amortization. A five-year term on a 25-year mortgage buys five years of certainty. After that, you renew the remaining debt at whatever terms are available.

Variable mortgages come in two forms. With an **adjustable payment**, the amount you pay changes with the rate. In these examples, the payment is recalculated to repay the loan by its original end date. With a **fixed payment**, the rate changes but the scheduled payment initially stays put: more interest means less principal repaid.

That second arrangement can feel more secure than it is. If the rate rises far enough, the payment may stop covering the interest. The lender's trigger provisions then matter. Depending on the contract, you may need a higher payment, a lump sum, or another adjustment. The Bank of Canada's [explanation of trigger rates](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2022/11/staff-analytical-notes-2022-19/) describes why a fixed payment does not eliminate exposure to higher rates.

Before comparing offers, establish which product you have, whether it is open or closed, what you can prepay, and what it costs to leave. A lower rate attached to different restrictions is a different bargain.

## Work out what your household can carry

Start with take-home income and subtract the spending you need to maintain: food, utilities, childcare, other loan payments, property tax, insurance, maintenance, and any savings contributions you consider essential. The amount left has to cover the mortgage. Gross income is useful to a lender; it is not the amount available in your bank account.

Then run the budget at a higher payment. In our example, if variable rises from 4% to 6% after the first year, the payment increases from $3,156 to about $3,816. That is another $660 every month. If the only way to pay it is to stop retirement contributions, that is a real cost even if you never miss a mortgage payment.

Savings help, but distinguish money available for this risk from money already committed to a renovation, taxes, or a car replacement. Home equity and a volatile investment portfolio are not equivalent to cash you can spend during an income interruption. Paying down the mortgage reduces future interest, but it also puts cash somewhere you may need the lender's permission to retrieve.

Income stability needs similar scrutiny. Two salaries provide less diversification if both depend on the same employer or industry. A self-employed person should test a weak earning year. Someone approaching retirement should use their expected retirement income. Parental leave and childcare are foreseeable budget changes, not remote emergencies.

This is consistent with [Campbell and Cocco's research on household mortgage choice](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8363-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b): mortgage size, income risk, and risk aversion change the value of payment certainty. Their model concerns US mortgages, so its contract details do not transfer directly to Canada. The useful lesson is that the household belongs inside the analysis.

Comfort with risk matters too. There is value in being able to ignore the next rate announcement. But comfort cannot make a payment affordable, and anxiety alone cannot tell you how much certainty is worth. Calculate the exposure first.

## What the market has already priced

Variable rates generally move with the lender's prime rate, which is influenced by the Bank of Canada's policy rate. Fixed offers depend on longer-term funding costs, including bond-market conditions. Those markets already reflect expectations about future policy, inflation, and growth. Lender funding costs, competition, and the features of the loan also affect the quote. The Bank of Canada explains these connections in [“What's behind your mortgage rate.”](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2020/05/whats-behind-your-mortgage-rate/)

Consequently, “rates are going to fall” is an incomplete reason to choose variable. How far, how soon, and from what starting rate? If variable starts above fixed, it may take substantial cuts just to recover the extra interest paid in the meantime.

Nor is the difference between today's two quotes simply an insurance premium. The rates cover different periods of exposure and reflect different expected paths and contract features. Fixed can even start below variable. Calling it the more expensive option before doing the calculation assumes the conclusion.

There is an economic reason variable can cost less: the borrower accepts more immediate interest-rate exposure. [Moshe Milevsky's historical study](https://wowa.ca/static/fixed-vs-variable-study.pdf) found substantial advantages to borrowing short in its sample. But the much-repeated historical success rates depend on the periods, rates, and strategies examined. They are not probabilities for a mortgage signed today. The report itself distinguishes historical outcomes from its forward-looking simulations.

A more recent [Canadian mortgage-choice model by Boutros, Clara, and Kartashova](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/03/staff-working-paper-2026-2/) considers fixed, adjustable-payment, and fixed-payment variable contracts together. Their results connect the appeal of each to financial buffers, leverage, and market pricing. They also show why term length matters: protection that expires in five years has a different value from protection lasting the life of the loan.

Use economic conditions to choose tests, rather than to manufacture confidence in one forecast. Persistent inflation is a reason to test higher payments and living costs together. Weak demand is a reason to test lower rates alongside lost income. Neither relationship is guaranteed, which is precisely why both scenarios deserve attention.

## What five years of different rates would cost

We can compare the offers without pretending to know which path will occur. Hold the mortgage amount, amortization, and five-year period constant. Change only the variable-rate path.

The first comparison uses adjustable-payment variable, so each payment keeps the loan on its original repayment timetable. Both offers use monthly payments and semi-annual compounding for consistency. Your lender's variable-rate convention may differ.

<MortgageRatePlanner section="outcomes" />

At a constant 4%, variable incurs about $111,691 in interest over five years, compared with $126,029 for fixed. The difference is about $14,338. This is interest saved, not the sum of the differences between payments: part of every payment reduces debt, and the mortgages finish with different balances.

If variable falls to 3% in month 13, the interest bill falls to about $89,328. If the same cut arrives in month 49, it is about $106,425. Both borrowers finish at 3%; one has paid roughly $17,100 more interest. A forecast that only gets the eventual rate right can still be unhelpful.

The sustained increase is more expensive. A rise to 6% in month 13 produces about $156,823 of interest, roughly $30,794 more than fixed. The payment also rises to $3,816. Those are separate consequences: a higher borrowing cost over the term and a larger claim on income immediately.

A temporary spike is revealing. If the rate rises to 6% for one year and then returns to 4%, variable still incurs about $2,498 less interest than fixed over the full five years. Yet the borrower has to make twelve of those higher payments. A mortgage can win the five-year cost comparison and still be difficult to live with during year two.

None of these paths has an assigned probability. Averaging them would not produce a meaningful expected cost. They establish what particular outcomes require of the borrower.

### How much would rates have to change?

A break-even calculation asks a narrower, answerable question: at what rate would variable's total interest equal fixed's, given a specified date for one permanent rate change?

<MortgageRatePlanner section="thresholds" />

Starting at 4%, variable could rise to about 4.64% in month 13 and still finish close to the fixed offer's five-year interest cost. Starting at 5%, it would need to fall to about 4.36% at that date. If that higher starting rate lasted until month 49, the remaining year's rate would have to fall to about 2.30% to catch up.

These are break-even conditions under a deliberately simple path. They make the offer comparison concrete without claiming to predict what the Bank of Canada will do.

## The same offers, five different households

Now put the payments into a household budget. The next chart saves monthly surpluses and uses that cash to cover deficits. It includes expenses beyond the mortgage and shows the first month that cash runs out. Below zero, the line records an unmet funding need; it does not assume the family can keep borrowing.

<MortgageRatePlanner section="households" />

**A household with a large buffer.** With $11,000 of monthly take-home income, $6,000 of other expenses, and $60,000 in cash, both offers remain manageable through the sustained rate increase. Variable becomes more expensive in that scenario, but it does not force an immediate spending crisis. This household has room to consider the potential savings in the other paths.

**A family taking parental leave.** The example starts with $9,000 of take-home income, $5,200 of other expenses, and $20,000 in savings. Income drops by $3,000 for twelve months. Both mortgages exhaust the available cash in month 16. Fixed reduces the maximum additional funding needed to about $7,375, compared with $8,653 under adjustable variable, but neither fits the plan as entered.

That result is useful. Choosing fixed would improve this family's position without solving the underlying shortfall. They need more savings before leave, lower spending, a smaller mortgage, or another credible source of income. A mortgage label cannot fill the gap.

**A household with commission income.** With somewhat more initial savings and a smaller income reduction, the weak-year example gets through both mortgages. Cash nevertheless falls from $25,000 to roughly $7,225 under fixed and $6,232 under adjustable variable. A repair or a longer slump could change the answer. The relevant question is how much of that remaining buffer the household is willing to put at risk.

**Someone losing a job during a recession.** Rate cuts help the variable borrower in the example, but monthly income falls by $4,000 while the mortgage payment eventually falls by only about $306. Variable delays the first funding shortfall from month 13 to month 14. Lower rates provide relief; they do not replace the lost salary.

**A borrower retiring next year.** In this example, retirement income is $7,000 a month and other expenses are $4,000. Fixed requires a cash draw of about $321 a month. Variable, after the assumed increase, requires about $816. Existing savings make either possible for a time. The decision still needs a longer retirement budget, including renewal: the fixed payment's protection ends after the term.

These cases also explain why cost rankings cannot be applied mechanically. A borrower with ample cash can accept an expensive year that would force another borrower to cut essential spending. The difference is a household constraint, not a disagreement about arithmetic.

## A fixed payment can postpone the problem

Fixed-payment variable deserves its own comparison because it can make a rate increase hard to see in the monthly budget.

Suppose the variable rate rises from 4% to 5.5% after one year. In this example, the original $3,156 payment still covers the interest, so it stays unchanged. Less of it reduces the mortgage. At the end of five years, the remaining debt is about $558,818, compared with $532,648 under adjustable payments and $526,778 under fixed.

<MortgageRatePlanner section="renewal" />

If all three renew at 5.5% over the remaining 20 years, the fixed-payment variable borrower needs about $3,824 a month. The adjustable borrower is already paying about $3,645. The fixed borrower goes from about $3,321 to $3,605.

The lower earlier payments had value: they left cash available for other uses. They also left more debt. To judge the trade fairly, look at both cash retained and the balance to be refinanced. If the spare cash was spent, it is no longer available to soften renewal.

A larger rate increase could invoke the lender's trigger provisions earlier. The calculator below assumes the payment is reset to restore the original amortization once it no longer covers interest. That is one explicit illustrative rule, not a promise about how a particular bank will handle your mortgage.

## A move can change the answer before rates do

A five-year contract deserves extra scrutiny if you might sell, refinance for renovations, separate, or move for work before it ends. Portability may help, but its conditions need to fit the transaction you actually expect. Prepayment privileges also matter if you intend to use a bonus or other cash inflow to reduce the loan.

Fixed closed-mortgage penalties can depend on an interest rate differential. The lender's calculation may use posted rates, discounts, and a comparison term. A simple estimate can therefore differ substantially from a payout statement. [FCAC's guide to prepayment penalties](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/mortgages/reduce-prepayment-penalties.html) explains the moving parts.

<MortgageRatePlanner section="exit" />

Under the default assumptions—a sale after 24 payments and a 3% comparison rate—the five-year fixed produces about $52,350 of interest and a simplified penalty of $25,769. The combined cost is about $78,119. The variable example, which falls to 3% in month 13, totals about $45,040 including its estimated penalty. These are results of the stated assumptions, not penalty quotes.

A shorter fixed term offers another choice. The example includes three years at 4.3%. It has a smaller estimated exit penalty at month 24, but renews sooner if you stay. Move the sale date past month 36 and change its renewal rate to see that exposure. All alternatives are measured to the same sale date; comparing three years of one loan with five years of another would conceal it.

The ability to convert variable to fixed later also needs a realistic price. You will be choosing from the terms available then. Today's fixed quote does not remain on standby while you wait to see whether variable works out.

## Compare your own offers

Use the calculator to test a budget you could actually maintain. Start with the unchanged-rate case, then try an increase, an income interruption, and an earlier exit. For each, inspect the interest and fees, peak payment, remaining debt, and lowest cash reserve together.

<MortgageRatePlanner section="calculator" />

Fixed is easier to justify when payment certainty protects spending you cannot readily cut, the term fits your plans, and the offer's price is acceptable across the scenarios you consider plausible. Variable is easier to justify when you can absorb the payments, have cash available for interruptions, and find its potential savings and contract flexibility worth the exposure.

If both offers leave a funding gap under a foreseeable event, revisit the budget or mortgage amount. If both are comfortably affordable, you have a real choice about cost, flexibility, and how much attention you want to give your mortgage. You do not need to know the future to make that choice carefully.

## Calculation notes

All amounts are Canadian dollars. The examples use monthly payments in arrears, no prepayments, and nominal annual rates compounded semi-annually for every product. Actual variable mortgages may use a different convention. The monthly rate is `(1 + annual rate / 2)^(1/6) − 1`, with the annual rate expressed as a decimal. Payments are calculated from the current balance, monthly rate, and remaining amortization. Calculations retain full precision; displayed dollars are rounded.

Interest is the borrowing cost. Principal repayment reduces debt. As a check, total payments plus the ending balance minus the original loan equals accumulated interest. Exit penalties are added separately. The comparisons are nominal totals, not discounted present values, and exclude returns on retained cash. They do not establish an investment or net-worth ranking.

Household projections hold expenses constant and save all monthly surplus without interest. Income reductions are after tax and any benefits. The first negative cash position marks a need for additional funding; subsequent mortgage payments remain a contractual projection, not a simulation of default. Property sale proceeds, mortgage payout funding, and exit penalties are excluded from the cash-reserve chart and must be considered separately at sale.

Renewal and break-even calculations use the specific future rates shown. The model excludes lender-specific daily interest, insurance premiums added to principal unless included in the entered balance, discharge and legal fees, taxes on investment withdrawals, inflation, missed-payment charges, and changes to borrowing eligibility. None of the rate or household scenarios is assigned a probability. Use the lender's written payment schedule and payout estimate to check an actual offer.
