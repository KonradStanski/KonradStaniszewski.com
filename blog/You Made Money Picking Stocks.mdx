Suppose you put $10,000 into a handful of stocks. A year later, you have $12,000. You followed the news, selected promising companies, and earned a 20% return.

If the same money in a broad market fund would have become $13,000, your decisions left you $1,000 behind the alternative. You made money by owning stocks, while choosing those particular stocks cost you money relative to the fund.

**The case for stock picking rests on the extra return your decisions produce.** That means improving on the diversified alternative after accounting for the risks and costs of your strategy.

Casual investors have little reason to expect that advantage. A company's promising future already influences its price. Professional investors compete to identify bargains. Choosing a few companies adds risks that diversification removes, while frequent trading adds costs. Together, these make a low-cost diversified fund a better starting point for long-term stock investing.

## Stock valuation and future cash flows

A share is a small ownership stake in a business. To understand what it is worth, start with what owning the business could eventually pay you.

Imagine owning a restaurant. Customers spend money there, but you cannot take all those sales home. You have to pay for ingredients, wages, rent, taxes, equipment, and any money the business owes. You might also use some of what remains to open another location. Your financial benefit as an owner depends on the cash the business can ultimately leave for you after meeting those needs.

Owning shares works the same way. A company can distribute cash to shareholders through dividends or by buying back their shares. It can also invest in the business to generate more cash later. That future earning capacity explains why a young company can be valuable while losing money today: investors expect its eventual cash generation to justify the money committed now.

Money is worth more now than later because money you have now can be invested and earn a return. This is the **time value of money**. Using an illustrative 10% annual return from a market index as a benchmark, $100 invested today would grow to $110 in a year and $121 in two years. Waiting two years to receive the same $100 means giving up those two years of potential growth.

*Discounting* accounts for that forgone return when valuing future money today. The longer you have to wait for a payment, the less it is worth today. Uncertainty matters too. For a riskier stream of payments, investors generally require a higher expected return, which lowers the price they are willing to pay today.

This is the idea behind valuing a stock using its [**discounted future cash flows**](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/val.html). Estimate the cash that can ultimately go to shareholders, when it will arrive, and the return required for taking the risk. Discount those future amounts back to today and add them up. Each share represents a portion of that estimated value.

You can also make money by selling your shares. The economic basis for what the next buyer will pay is the cash they expect ownership to produce from that point onward. The value of the sale therefore rests on the same underlying business prospects.

Investors use these forecasts to decide what they will pay, and their trading establishes the market price. More expected cash, earlier payments, or lower risk raise the estimated value of a share. Weaker prospects, longer waits, or a higher required return lower it. The number of shares matters as well, because issuing more can dilute each existing share's ownership stake.

## Price discovery and competition

A valuation becomes relevant to the market when someone is willing to trade on it. Buyers submit *bids*, the prices they will pay, and sellers submit *offers*, the prices they will accept. A trade occurs when an order meets an available price. The latest completed trade becomes the quoted last price. The SEC's [guide to stock quotes](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/stock-quotes) explains these terms.

Suppose a stock is trading at $100. The company announces a major contract, and an investment firm's analysts revise their estimate of its value to $120 per share. The fund wants to buy at the current price. For a simple example, suppose these shares are available for sale:

| Asking price | Shares available |
| --- | ---: |
| $100 | 100 |
| $101 | 200 |
| $102 | 300 |

A market order to buy 300 shares takes the 100 shares offered at $100 and the next 200 at $101. The latest trade is now at $101, and the cheapest remaining shares are offered at $102. Buying has used up the lower-priced offers. This is how an order can execute at [several different prices](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/types-orders).

Other investors analyzing the contract can also raise their bids, while sellers raise the prices they will accept. As the stock becomes more expensive, the gap between its purchase price and the analysts' estimate shrinks. Bad news works in the other direction: investors lower their bids, and sellers willing to accept those lower prices drive trades downward.

This process is **price discovery**. Information changes investors' expectations, those expectations change their orders, and the orders change the prices at which shares trade. The new contract can affect the share price immediately, years before it produces the cash investors expect.

> “I follow the industry closely and read the company's financial reports.”

The price available to you is the product of buying and selling across the market. It reflects information and judgments acted on by individual investors, pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds, and trading firms. **The assessment you need to improve on is the one already embedded in that market price.**

That competition includes sophisticated hedge funds with specialist analysts, proprietary data, and substantial research budgets. It also includes market makers and high-frequency trading firms using [direct exchange feeds and automated systems](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/2014-spch060514mjw) to react rapidly to changes in market conditions. Research shapes investors' estimates of value; trading turns those estimates and incoming information into the prices available to everyone else.

An *edge* is a repeatable advantage that improves investment decisions enough to survive their additional costs and risks. Claiming one means expecting to identify mistakes in a price formed through this collective activity, consistently enough to profit from correcting them. The challenge is to add something valuable beyond the combined research, information, and trading already influencing the price.

Familiarity with a product, knowledge of an industry, and time spent reading public reports do not establish that advantage. The same evidence is available to a vast pool of competing investors, including professionals whose full-time work is finding and exploiting mispriced stocks. When they act, the opportunity changes. A casual investor has no sound basis for expecting ordinary public research to beat that process consistently.

## Growth expectations and the purchase price

> “I expect this company to grow substantially over the next few years.”

Investors value a growing company using forecasts that extend years into the future. The price can therefore rise in anticipation of expansion, long before the sales arrive. Buying at that price means paying for the expected growth in advance.

Suppose the restaurant's purchase price assumes it will become a chain of twenty profitable locations. Opening the second location is progress toward the future you paid for. Its investment value depends on whether that expansion remains on course, how much it costs, and how much cash the completed chain will generate. Counting new locations alone tells you very little about the return on your purchase.

In a simplified stock example, suppose the price reflects an expectation that profit will grow by 50%. Profit then grows by 30%. The business has improved, but the result disappoints the expectations behind the purchase price. Holding other things equal, the stock can fall. A struggling company can experience the reverse: things go less badly than expected, and its stock rises.

<ExpectationsComparison />

This is what “priced in” means: the purchase price already reflects expectations about the future. A company can grow as expected and reward investors for holding it. An unusually high return requires a more favorable outcome than the one built into the price.

### Snap's revenue growth and shareholder returns

Snap reported annual revenue of [$58.7 million in 2015 and $404.5 million in 2016](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1564408/000119312517029199/d270216ds1.htm). By 2025, annual revenue had reached [$5.93 billion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1564408/000156440826000013/snap-20251231.htm). That is roughly a hundredfold increase from 2015.

Snap's shares were [offered at $17 in its March 2017 IPO](https://investor.snap.com/news/news-details/2017/Snap-Inc-Announces-Pricing-of-Initial-Public-Offering/default.aspx) and [closed 2025 at $8.07](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1564408/000156440826000013/snap-20251231.htm). Annual revenue reached nearly fifteen times its pre-IPO level while the share price fell by more than half.

An investor buying at the IPO price could have correctly predicted enormous revenue growth and still lost more than half the investment by the end of 2025. Shareholders' returns depend on how much growth they paid for, how that growth translates into cash available to them, and how that cash is divided among shares.

Predicting growth is only part of valuing a business. The investment decision also requires judging whether the price leaves an attractive return for the buyer.

## Past performance and future returns

> “The stock has performed well for several years, and I expect that to continue.”

A stock's past return measures the gain from buying at an earlier price. A new investment begins at today's price, with today's expectations about future cash flows.

Past gains can make that purchase harder to justify. If a stock rose because investors became more optimistic, a new buyer pays more for that optimism. Repeating the earlier return requires the business's prospects to justify further gains from the higher price.

In November 2020, [an article recommended Zoom, DocuSign, Teladoc, Wayfair, and Peloton as likely winners in 2021](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/11/16/zoom-docusign-teladoc-wayfair-and-peloton-will-als/). Each had already risen between 119% and 493% that year. The businesses had recognizable products, rapid growth, and an appealing story about how life was changing.

Their earlier success offered no protection against buying at a price that subsequent performance failed to justify. Past returns describe a completed investment opportunity. They do not establish the value of the opportunity available now.

## Measuring returns against a benchmark

The purpose of choosing stocks is to improve the return on your savings. The alternative is readily available: a broad index fund spreads ownership across many companies and gives you a share of their collective performance. You can hold it without deciding which individual companies will do best.

A comparable fund therefore provides a **benchmark**: the return available from a practical alternative to managing the stocks yourself.

Compare your **whole portfolio** with that benchmark over the same dates, including dividends and costs and accounting for money added or withdrawn along the way. Every holding belongs in the comparison, with gains and losses weighted by the money invested.

Risk belongs in the comparison too. Borrowing money or concentrating in one industry can produce larger gains when conditions are favorable and larger losses when they reverse. A strategy has to justify the additional risk as well as the work it requires.

## Evidence from retail investors and active funds

### Retail trading and costs

The same distinction between making money and improving on the market appears in studies of individual investors. Frequent trading adds another expense to a selection process that already lacks a demonstrated advantage.

[Barber and Odean studied the stock investments of 66,465 households](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/Individual_Investor_Performance_Final.pdf) at a U.S. discount broker during 1991–1996. The average household earned 16.4% a year after trading costs, while the market earned 17.9%. The most active traders earned 11.4%.

The average investor earned a substantial positive return while still falling behind the market. That is the opening example in practice: a growing account can conceal the cost of the decisions made along the way.

<RetailPerformanceEvidence />

For the 62,439 households with more than a year of records, 49.3% beat the market in average monthly return before trading costs; 43.4% did afterward. The most active investors incurred the largest penalties from the commissions and spreads they paid.

<RetailActivityEvidence />

Even before those costs, more trading produced no meaningful improvement in performance. The extra activity increased the amount investors paid without improving the investments they held.

More recent evidence connects stock selection to the appeal of popular companies. A [2022 study of Robinhood users](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13183) found that the stocks subject to the most intense daily buying subsequently returned an average of 4.7 percentage points below the study's benchmark over 20 trading days. The attention drawing investors to these stocks failed to translate into better returns.

### Professional fund performance

Professional managers face the same difficulty. [Fama and French studied 3,156 active U.S. equity funds over 1984–2006](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01598.x). Their tests suggested that few funds generated enough return beyond their benchmarks to cover their costs.

The chart compares estimated alpha: the return left after adjusting for exposure to broad market factors. Across the four combinations of fund weighting and benchmark model shown here, the average estimate was close to zero before fund expenses and negative afterward.

The authors also compared actual fund results with simulated results in which no fund had an edge. This accounts for the lucky winners you would expect in a large population. They found some evidence of skill before expenses, but little evidence that investors could capture superior returns after paying them.

</TechnicalNote>

Research is expensive, and competing investors are trying to capture the same opportunities. Even a professional operation has difficulty producing an advantage large enough to cover its costs. Casual familiarity with a company is a poor basis for expecting to do better.

<TechnicalNote title="The arithmetic of active management">

[William Sharpe's arithmetic of active management](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/active/active.htm) makes the competition explicit. Define passive investors as those who hold every stock in a market in proportion to its market value. Their holdings mirror the market, so the remaining holdings of active investors, taken together, must also mirror it.

Before costs, the average active dollar therefore earns the market return. Active investors' gains relative to the market are offset by other active investors' shortfalls. If active investing costs more than the comparable passive approach, the average active dollar keeps less afterward.

</TechnicalNote>

## Concentration, risk, and luck

> “My stock picks have beaten the market.”

Individual winners fit this pattern. Investors holding different combinations of stocks get different results, and concentration magnifies the differences. Some portfolios will outperform by a large margin even when their owners have no advantage in selecting investments.

If you put half your money into one company, a doubling of its share price lifts your entire portfolio by 50%, assuming the other half stays flat. A collapse in that company damages your savings on the same scale. Concentration makes the outcome depend heavily on one business without improving your ability to predict it.

Some amber paths finish far above the blue index paths, entirely through chance. The two strategies have the same expected ending value before extra costs, but the picker's outcomes spread much farther apart. Most finish below their paired index, while a small number of very large outcomes pull up the average.

Looking only at the successful pickers would hide this distribution. A long winning streak also needs context: several bets on companies in the same booming industry may all benefit from the same conditions. Evidence of skill requires a process that adds value across many decisions and different market conditions, measured against the risks taken.

A broad stock fund exposes you to recessions and market crashes. Investors demand an expected return for committing money through that uncertainty. Concentration adds risks that spreading your money across companies can reduce: a failed product, fraud, a lost customer, or a competitor taking over. The market has no reason to offer you extra return for bearing a risk you could diversify away.

## Diversification and the distribution of market returns

> “I'd rather invest in a few companies I know well than own the whole market.”

The market's long-term gains depend heavily on a small number of exceptionally successful companies. A concentrated portfolio risks missing those companies altogether.

[Hendrik Bessembinder studied U.S. stocks from 1926–2016](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2900447). Four out of seven stocks returned less over their listed lifetimes than one-month Treasury bills. Just 4.31% of companies accounted for all the market's net wealth creation above that Treasury-bill return. Among the remaining companies, the gains and losses relative to Treasury bills canceled out.

<WealthCreationIllustration />

Diversification therefore serves two purposes: it limits the damage from individual failures and helps ensure you participate in the exceptional successes.

A broad total-market fund holds the winners as well as the disappointments. As successful companies grow in market value, they become a larger part of a fund weighted by market value. You get to participate without having to know in advance which few companies will matter most.

Trying to capture those exceptional gains with a few stocks requires identifying the winners in advance and holding them through their growth. Diversification lets the portfolio benefit from them without depending on that prediction.

## The case for diversified investing

A low-cost diversified fund gives you a practical way to invest in business growth. It includes successful companies alongside those whose prospects disappoint, spreads company-specific risks, and requires little ongoing work. For the portion of long-term savings allocated to stocks, those are substantial advantages.

Casual stock picking replaces that broad ownership with a forecast about which companies will deliver better returns at today's prices. The forecast competes with professional research, adds exposure to individual failures, and often leads to trading that reduces what the investor keeps. Following an industry or recognizing a strong business does not establish the advantage needed to justify those costs and risks.

The standard for choosing individual stocks is a demonstrated ability to improve on the diversified alternative. Until there is evidence of that ability, making stock selection central to an investment plan means making the plan depend on an advantage the investor has no reason to expect.

Owning a broad fund lets you earn the returns of the businesses in it without having to outguess the investors setting their prices. You can benefit from companies succeeding while leaving the competition to identify underpriced stocks to others.
